Why window film fits St. Louis City buildings

The City's housing stock is old, brick and full of glass. Soulard is packed with red-brick row houses, many dating to the mid- to late-1800s. Lafayette Square's Victorian townhouses, in styles from Second Empire to Queen Anne, face Lafayette Park, which opened in 1851. In Southampton, most homes went up between the 1920s and late 1930s, brick and stone bungalows and Tudor cottages. Dogtown mixes early-1900s single-family bungalows with multi-family flats.

Those buildings share a few traits that make film a good fit. Many still have tall original windows or single-pane glass that lets in a lot of heat in July and a lot of glare in the late afternoon. Brick holds heat, so a west-facing room can stay warm well into the evening. And plenty of owners want to keep their original sash rather than replace it, which is where film shines: it works on the glass you already have.

Downtown is a different animal. The Washington Avenue Historic District is a former garment district of 55 commercial buildings built between 1899 and 1931, many now converted to residential lofts. Big factory windows are the selling point of a loft, and they are also why lofts overheat, fade floors and turn screens unreadable at certain hours.

Local note: historic districts and the Cultural Resources Office

The City has 18 local historic districts, including Soulard, Lafayette Square, the Central West End, Shaw, Benton Park, Fox Park and Tower Grove East. In those districts, exterior changes are reviewed against each district's standards by the City's Cultural Resources Office. Window film is applied to the inside of the glass and can be removed, so it is usually treated differently from replacing windows, but rules vary by district and a mirrored or dark film can change how a facade reads from the street. Check with the Cultural Resources Office about your address first. We will show you neutral, low-reflectivity films that keep the windows looking original.

Window film options for City homes and businesses

Homes, flats and row houses

For brick homes with original windows, our residential window tinting starts with the problem room: the sunroom that bakes, the bedroom that never cools, the front parlor with fading floors. A solar heat control window film on the south and west glass handles most of it. Light ceramic and spectrally selective films reject heat while staying close to clear, which matters on a historic facade. If you are weighing new sash instead, read our comparison of window film vs. replacement windows first.

Older single-pane glass also loses heat in winter. A low-E window film reflects some of that heat back into the room, which helps drafty-feeling front rooms in January without changing the windows themselves.

Lofts and condos

Loft windows are huge and often face another building across the street. That makes privacy window film and glare control the usual asks. Daytime privacy films let you see out while making it harder to see in during the day, and frosted film on lower panes works around the clock. Ask your association what is allowed before you commit.

Storefronts and offices

Neighborhood storefronts on streets in Soulard, the Central West End, Tower Grove and Dogtown deal with displays that fade and front counters that turn into greenhouses. Our commercial window tinting pairs a neutral solar film with UV fade protection (most quality films block up to 99% of UV) so the merchandise stays visible and holds its color longer. Where smash-and-grab is a worry, security and safety window film helps hold broken glass together longer.
