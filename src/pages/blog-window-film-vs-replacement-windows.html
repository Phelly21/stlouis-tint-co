If your window frames are solid and the glass seals are intact, window film is usually the smarter buy: it cuts heat, glare and UV fading and adds privacy for far less money and disruption than new windows. If your windows have rotted sills, fogged glass between the panes, bad drafts or broken hardware, replacement is the right fix, because film only treats the glass. Many St. Louis homes end up doing both, replacing the worst units and filming the rest.

Start with the real question: what problem are you trying to solve?

Homeowners usually come to this decision from one of two directions. Either a room is uncomfortable (the west-facing living room that bakes every July afternoon, the bedroom that glares at sunrise, the hardwood floor that keeps fading), or the windows themselves are wearing out. Those are different problems with different fixes.

Window film works on the glass. It changes how much solar heat, visible light and ultraviolet energy passes through. Replacement windows fix the whole unit: frame, sash, seals, weatherstripping, hardware and glass. If your complaint is about what comes through the glass, film is often enough. If your complaint is about air, water or a window that won't open, film can't help.

When replacement windows are the right call

We'd rather tell you to replace a window than put film on one that's failing. These are the signs that point toward replacement:

  • Rot or soft wood in the sill, sash or frame. Push a screwdriver into the bottom rail; if it sinks in, the window has a structural problem.
  • Fogging or condensation between the panes of double-pane glass. That means the seal has failed and the insulating gas is gone. Film can't reseal it.
  • Drafts you can feel around the sash or frame, especially on cold, windy days. Air leakage is a frame and weatherstripping issue, not a glass issue.
  • Broken hardware: sashes that won't stay up, cranks that strip, locks that don't latch, or windows painted shut beyond saving.
  • Water getting in during storms, or staining on the wall below the window.
  • Cracked or broken glass. Film shouldn't go on damaged glass; the pane needs replacing first.

If several of these describe your windows, the money is better spent on new units. You can always add film later for privacy or extra UV protection.

When window film makes more sense

If the frames are sound and the windows open, close and seal properly, the problems most people actually feel day to day are about sunlight. That's where film does its best work:

  • Heat gain. St. Louis summers are hot and humid, and south- and west-facing glass can turn a room into a greenhouse by late afternoon. Solar heat control window film rejects a share of that solar energy before it heats the room.
  • Glare on TVs, computer screens and kitchen counters.
  • Fading. Quality films block up to 99% of UV, which is a major driver of fading on floors, rugs, furniture and artwork. See our UV and fade protection film options.
  • Privacy on street-facing rooms, bathrooms and sidelights, without blocking all the light. Our privacy window film page covers the choices.
  • Safety. Security and safety film helps hold broken glass together, something a standard replacement window doesn't add.

Film also goes on in a day or less for most homes, with no tear-out, no trim repair, no painting and no dumpster in the driveway. You keep your windows, your interior trim and your schedule.

Older single-pane glass: where low-E film earns its keep

A lot of St. Louis housing still has original single-pane windows, especially in the City and in inner-ring suburbs where homes went up long before insulated glass was common. Single-pane glass loses heat fast in winter and lets heat pour in during summer.

Replacing every one of those windows is a big project. A middle path is low-E window film, which adds a thin, low-emissivity coating to the glass. It reflects radiant heat back into the room in cold weather and rejects solar heat in warm weather. It won't make a single-pane window perform like a new insulated unit, and it won't stop drafts around a loose sash, but paired with storm windows or good weatherstripping it can noticeably improve comfort. Performance varies by film, and we show you the spec sheet (TSER, VLT, U-value where listed) before you choose.

Historic homes: keeping the original windows

In older neighborhoods across St. Louis City, Webster Groves and Kirkwood, original wood windows are part of what gives a house its character, and some homes sit in historic districts with their own rules about exterior changes. Film goes on the interior side of the glass and leaves the frames, muntins and exterior look alone. If your home is in a designated district, check with your local preservation office before changing anything visible from the street. We can show you films with a neutral, non-mirrored look that's hard to spot from outside.

The combination approach most homes actually need

It's rarely all or nothing. A typical house has a few windows in rough shape and a lot of windows that are fine. A practical plan looks like this:

  1. Triage the windows. Walk the house and note any with rot, fogged glass, drafts or broken hardware. Those go on the replacement list.
  2. Find the sun problems. Note which rooms overheat, glare or fade. West and south exposures are the usual suspects in St. Louis.
  3. Replace only what's failing. Spend replacement money where film can't help.
  4. Film the sound glass. Treat the hot, bright or exposed rooms with the right film for each one.
  5. Revisit new units later. If new windows still glare or need privacy, film can often be added, once you've checked that window's warranty terms.
