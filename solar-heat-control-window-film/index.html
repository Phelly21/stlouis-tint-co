Solar Heat Control Window Film in St. Louis

Solar heat control window film is a film applied to your glass that rejects a share of the sun's infrared heat and visible light before it warms the room. It's the go-to fix for west- and south-facing rooms that overheat every St. Louis summer afternoon. Ceramic, reflective and nearly clear spectrally selective films all work, and the right one depends on your glass, the look you want, and how much heat you need to block.

How heat control film works

Sunlight delivers energy in three bands: ultraviolet, visible light and infrared. Infrared is the part you feel as heat on your skin through a sunny window. Heat control films use dyes, metal layers or ceramic particles to absorb or reflect part of that energy before it passes into the room.

Films also block up to 99% of UV, which is the main driver of fading on floors, furniture and merchandise. Exactly how much heat and light a film stops varies by film, so we show you the spec sheet before you choose.

Signs you need heat control film

  • A room is several degrees hotter than the rest of the house or office every sunny afternoon.
  • The air conditioning runs constantly but one side of the building never cools down.
  • You can feel heat radiating off the glass when you sit near the window.
  • Blinds stay closed all summer just to keep the room bearable.
  • Workers near a glass wall complain about heat and glare in the afternoon.

The numbers on a spec sheet, in plain words

You don't need to be an engineer to compare films. Three numbers do most of the work:

  • TSER (total solar energy rejected): the share of the sun's total energy the film and glass keep out. Higher means more heat blocked. This is the best single number for heat control.
  • VLT (visible light transmitted): how much daylight gets through. A high VLT film looks light; a low VLT film looks darker and cuts more glare.
  • SHGC (solar heat gain coefficient): the share of solar heat that does get in, from 0 to 1. Lower is better for summer heat. It's roughly the flip side of TSER.

A good film lets you mix these: a ceramic or spectrally selective film can have a high VLT and still a solid TSER, meaning a bright room that stays cooler.

Interior vs. exterior-applied film

Most heat control film goes on the inside of the glass, where it's protected from weather and lasts longer. Exterior-applied film stops heat on the outside surface before the glass warms up, which can help in a few situations, like some insulated units or glass that's hard to reach from inside. The trade-off is exposure to rain, hail, pollen and window washing, so exterior films usually have a shorter life. We recommend one or the other after looking at your windows.

Dual-pane glass and thermal stress

Any film that absorbs heat warms the glass it's on. On most windows that's not an issue, but large insulated units, glass with partial shade lines from an overhang or tree, or certain tinted and older glass can be more sensitive to uneven heating. That's called thermal stress.

Why we check your glass first

During the estimate we identify whether glass is single-pane, dual-pane, tempered, laminated or already low-E coated, and look at window size, shading and orientation. Then we only recommend films that are compatible with that glass. If you're concerned about your window maker's warranty, see will window film void my window warranty?

Where heat control film is used

At home, it's most often used in west- and south-facing living rooms, sunrooms, bonus rooms over garages, skylights and patio doors. Our residential window tinting page covers room-by-room options. For businesses, it goes on office glass, lobbies, conference rooms, storefronts and any workstation near a sunny wall of windows; see commercial window tinting.

Heat control in St. Louis

St. Louis summers bring long, hot, humid stretches with strong afternoon sun. West-facing glass takes the hardest hit because the low sun hits it nearly straight on right when the day is hottest. Many brick homes in the City of St. Louis and Brentwood have one upstairs room that never cools off, while newer homes in Des Peres often have big rear-facing great-room windows.

On the business side, glass office buildings in Clayton, Creve Coeur and Maryland Heights deal with hot perimeter offices and glare on screens. Because winters here get cold too, some clients choose a low-E window film that blocks summer heat and holds heat in during winter.

Our installation process

  1. Estimate and samples on your glass. We measure, identify the glass type and put sample films on the windows that get the most sun.
  2. Film choice with the spec sheet. We compare TSER, VLT and glare reduction across a few compatible films, then give you a written quote.
  3. Glass prep and cleaning. Sills and floors are covered, and each pane is scraped and cleaned so nothing gets trapped under the film.
  4. Precise install and edge trim. Film is wet-applied, squeegeed flat and trimmed to a clean, even edge.
  5. Cure time and care instructions. Light haze or water pockets can last days to a few weeks while the film cures. We leave written care instructions.

What affects the cost

Cost depends on total glass area, the film category (ceramic and spectrally selective films cost more than dyed), interior vs. exterior application, window access and height, number of panes, and removal of any old film. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best window film for heat rejection?

It depends on how the room looks and what glass you have. Reflective films usually reject the most heat, while ceramic and spectrally selective films reject strong amounts of heat with a more natural look. We compare the TSER numbers of a few films on your glass so you can choose.

What does TSER mean on window film?

TSER stands for total solar energy rejected. It is the share of the sun's total energy, including visible light and infrared heat, that the film and glass keep out. A higher TSER means more heat blocked, and it is the best single number for comparing heat control films.

Can heat control film crack a dual-pane window?

Film adds some heat to the glass, and the wrong film on certain insulated or tempered-edge units can raise the risk of thermal stress. That is why a pro checks your glass type, size, shading and orientation first and picks a compatible film. It is a good reason not to guess with a store-bought roll.

Do heat control films make windows look dark or mirrored?

Only some of them. Reflective films have a mirror look from outside during the day, but ceramic and spectrally selective films stay neutral and can be nearly clear. We put samples on your windows so you can see the look before deciding.

Is interior or exterior window film better for heat?

Exterior-applied film stops heat before it enters the glass, which can be useful on certain glass types, but it is exposed to weather and usually has a shorter life. Interior film is protected, lasts longer and handles most homes and offices well. We recommend which one fits your windows during the estimate.

Will heat control film lower my cooling bill?

It can reduce the solar heat coming through treated windows, which may ease the load on your air conditioning, especially in sunny west- and south-facing rooms. Actual savings vary by film, glass, orientation and how your home or building is used, so we do not promise a specific number.
